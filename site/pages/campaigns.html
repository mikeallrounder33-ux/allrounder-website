Four campaign shapes

Campaigns fail in predictable ways depending on what you sell. A launch fails differently from a retail push. These are the four shapes we plan most often — each one a starting structure we adapt, not a package we sell off the shelf.

01  Product launch

The problem: a launch has one date and no second chance at a first impression. Most launches spend everything in week one, then go quiet exactly when interest peaks.

What the plan covers

  • Pre-launch audience building — list, waitlist and warm audiences before anything is announced
  • The announcement itself: sequencing across owned, paid and earned so they compound rather than overlap
  • Launch-week creative volume and the order it goes live in
  • A 90-day follow-through plan, budgeted before launch so it doesn't get cut
  • Stock, fulfilment and support readiness checks against forecast demand

Best when: you have a firm date, something genuinely new to say, and a quarter to prepare.

02  D2C & e-commerce growth

The problem: the product already sells. Scaling it profitably is a different question from making the first sales, and the tactics that got you here usually stop working somewhere around the point you notice.

What the plan covers

  • Offer architecture — entry product, bundles, and the path to a second order
  • Contribution margin by product and channel, so scale decisions are made on profit rather than revenue
  • Paid social and search structured for prospecting and retention separately
  • Email and SMS flows carrying the retention half of the economics
  • A creative production cadence matched to how fast the spend exhausts assets

Best when: you have consistent sales, real margin data, and want to scale without the cost per order following it up.

03  Local & retail

The problem: the addressable market is geographic, so national tactics waste most of the budget. Winning locally is a different discipline — closer to visibility and reputation than to reach.

What the plan covers

  • Local search: profile completeness, categories, and the queries that actually convert nearby
  • A review engine — how reviews get requested consistently rather than occasionally
  • Geo-targeted paid with radius and dayparting matched to real trading patterns
  • In-store or in-market events, and the calendar that fills them
  • Expansion logic: what has to be true before the next location or region

Best when: customers come from a defined area, and being findable at the moment of intent matters more than brand reach.

04  B2B demand generation

The problem: long cycles, several people involved in the decision, and lead volume that looks fine right up until sales tells you none of it closes.

What the plan covers

  • ICP definition and account mapping — who's worth pursuing, and who is quietly wasting the pipeline
  • A content engine aimed at the questions asked during evaluation, not at the top of the funnel
  • LinkedIn and search built for a considered purchase rather than an impulse one
  • Lead scoring and handover that sales will actually work
  • Measurement that survives a six-month cycle, where last-click attribution is meaningless

Best when: deals are large enough to justify a long cycle, and marketing and sales need to agree on what a good lead is.

Which one fits?

Often more than one, and the answer changes with the stage you're at — a launch becomes a growth campaign about ninety days in. The campaign planning engagement starts by establishing which shape you're actually in, which is not always the one you'd expect.
